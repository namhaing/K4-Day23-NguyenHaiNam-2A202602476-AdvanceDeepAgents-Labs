# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Methods, Evidence, and Evaluation

## TL;DR

- Reasoning RL optimizes sampled multi-step solutions using outcome checks, stepwise process feedback, or both; outcome labels are cheaper, while process rewards expose intermediate errors and improve trace quality [1][2].
- The field’s algorithmic pivot is from preference-based PPO with a learned reward model and value critic toward verifier-based methods such as GRPO, which removes the critic and uses within-prompt grouped rewards [3][4].
- GRPO yields measurable gains on three math benchmarks: DeepSeekMath-Instruct rose from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K, 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH, and 84.6% to 88.8% on CMATH [4].
- Evaluation is a weak link: contamination can make widely used math benchmark results unreliable, and VerifyBench reports substantial room for improvement on harder verifier instances [5][6].
- The central open challenge is not just maximizing benchmark scores: reward correctness, trace quality, generalization, sampling and verifier cost, and safety need to be measured together [7][8].

## Background

Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning is post-training in which a policy generates a solution—often a sequence of intermediate “thought” tokens—and receives reward for the generated trajectory, rather than learning only from fixed demonstrations. The reward can arrive at the end of the answer or at intermediate steps; this distinguishes reasoning RL from ordinary supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while also separating it from preference-based RLHF, where a learned model scores broad human preferences such as helpfulness [9]. The distinction matters because a final answer can be correct despite flawed reasoning, and because many math or code answers can be checked against an answer key, symbolic rule, compiler, or tests [2][10].

The inherited RLHF template is instructive. InstructGPT collects demonstrations, learns a reward model from human comparisons, then optimizes an SFT policy with PPO and a per-token KL penalty; on the authors’ prompt distribution, 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT outputs were preferred to 175B GPT-3 outputs [11]. Summarization RLHF similarly trained a preference reward model from 64,000+ comparisons, with PPO optimizing against a completed-summary score and KL regularization [3]. Reasoning RL keeps the policy-optimization idea but can replace subjective preference labels with task-specific correctness signals, or add process rewards to guide credit assignment [4][9]. This is important now because recent reports show strong math and coding gains from post-training, but demonstrate that those gains depend on what the reward and test set actually measure [10][5].

## Outcome rewards versus process rewards

Outcome supervision scores the final answer; process supervision scores intermediate reasoning steps. The trade-off is label cost versus diagnostic precision. On GSM8K, outcome-only supervision achieved similar final-answer error rates with less annotation, but low reasoning-trace error required process supervision or reward models trained to emulate it. The study reports final-answer error falling from 16.8% to 12.7%, and reasoning-trace error among correct solutions falling from 14.0% to 3.4% [1]. It also notes that final-answer-only supervision used 1–4 label tokens per question, compared with hundreds for full-trace SFT [1].

Process supervision offers finer credit assignment, but its labels are more demanding. “Let’s Verify Step by Step” reports that process supervision beat outcome supervision on a representative MATH test subset, with 78% solved, and released PRM800K with 800,000 step-level human-feedback labels [2]. Active learning increased process-supervision data efficiency by 2.6× [2]. Yet final-answer correctness is easier to automate for MATH, while step labels in that work required human annotation [2]. Thus, outcome rewards scale more naturally where answers are verifiable, while process rewards address intermediate errors but introduce judge quality and data-cost concerns. More recent approaches try to combine rather than choose: PRPO aligns process-reward scores with outcome advantages, reporting 64.4% on MATH500 for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B versus 61.2% for GRPO with eight rollouts, while warning that process rewards alone may suppress necessary but lower-scored steps [12].

## From preference PPO to verifier-based policy optimization

PPO-based RLHF uses a learned preference reward and a value model to estimate advantages, with KL regularization keeping the policy near its SFT starting point [11]. Reasoning RL can retain PPO while changing the reward to a verifier, but the extra critic can be expensive. DeepSeekMath introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), removing the separate critic and using the average reward of multiple answers to a prompt as the baseline [4]. In its reported results, GRPO improved DeepSeekMath-Instruct from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K, 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH, and 84.6% to 88.8% on CMATH [4]. The design therefore trades a learned value estimate for relative comparisons among sampled responses, fitting tasks where many candidate solutions can be checked.

DeepSeek-R1-Zero shows the high-upside version of this recipe: it begins from DeepSeek-V3-Base without preceding SFT, uses GRPO and final-answer correctness rewards, and does not directly constrain the reasoning process [10]. The authors report emergent self-reflection, verification, and strategy adaptation without human-labeled reasoning trajectories; this is evidence from a math-focused, verifiable reward setup, not evidence that the same reward generalizes to every reasoning domain [10]. The full DeepSeek-R1 pipeline differs materially from R1-Zero: it adds cold-start data, rejection sampling and SFT, then a further RL stage incorporating preference/reward-model feedback for general helpfulness and harmlessness [10]. The Hugging Face paper summary likewise describes R1 as a multi-stage RL approach, while providing no benchmark-specific scores itself [13].

## Beyond sparse outcome feedback: process models and exploration

A second family improves the information available to the policy. Process reward models (PRMs) assign feedback to steps, while outcome reward models (ORMs) score complete solutions; PRMs can support both test-time selection and RL updates [9]. PRM800K demonstrated large-scale human step annotation [2], while later work summarized on Hugging Face describes Monte Carlo estimation combined with an LLM judge to address biases in Best-of-N evaluation [14]. ReasonFlux-PRM adds trajectory-aware supervision to step-level evaluation and is described as usable for distillation, RL, and test-time scaling [15]. These methods target different failure modes: step rewards localize mistakes, whereas trajectory-level signals retain information about whether a complete chain succeeds [14][15]. They also make reward-model quality a direct part of the learning system.

Exploration-centered approaches instead change how useful trajectories are generated. Reinforcement Learning via Self-Play (RLSP) is summarized as decoupling exploration from correctness signals and reporting emergent backtracking and verification [16]. Agent-R uses iterative self-training with Monte Carlo Tree Search to help agents recover from errors in interactive settings [17]. These approaches contrast with straightforward verifier-driven GRPO: search or self-play may produce more diverse or corrected trajectories, but their summaries do not supply comparable benchmark scores, so the evidence is less quantitative than GRPO’s math results [4][16][17]. This difference makes evaluation design consequential: improvements in exploration must be separated from gains caused by extra sampling, search budget, or easier reward access.

## Evaluation: benchmark scores, verifier quality, and cost

The usual evaluation of reasoning RL reports task accuracy, but benchmark accuracy alone cannot establish whether a model learned a procedure or memorized examples. A 2025 contamination study argues that results on widely used math benchmarks can be unreliable because of contamination and introduces RandomCalculation, a generator of clean arithmetic problems with arbitrary length and difficulty [5][18]. The study recommends uncontaminated evaluation and, where feasible, multiple model families to test whether RL results generalize [18]. This motivates fresh, generated tests and cross-model checks rather than reliance on a single familiar test set [5][18].

The verifier itself also needs benchmarking. VerifyBench benchmarks reference-based reward systems and includes a harder split; its reported evaluation finds substantial room for improvement on challenging instances [6][7]. This cautions against assuming that a verifier that works on a standard test set will remain reliable on harder cases. RM-Bench complements this by testing reward models’ sensitivity to subtle content differences and style bias, a limitation that answer-key accuracy does not fully capture [19].

## Trends and open problems

The recent trajectory is from preference learning toward mixtures of verifiable outcome reward, process supervision, and inference-time search. In 2024, GRPO demonstrated critic-free relative updates with gains across three math benchmarks [4]; in 2025, R1-Zero was reported to elicit self-reflection and verification without human-written reasoning traces, while the broader R1 pipeline reincorporated SFT and preference-oriented reward components [10]. Kimi k1.5 is also described as scaling RL with long-context and policy-optimization methods and strong results across reasoning benchmarks, but its HF summary gives no task-level numbers, limiting direct comparison [20]. Thus, the field’s public headline results outpace the comparability of its evidence.

Several issues remain unresolved. First, reward hacking and verifier errors can make optimization actively harmful: contamination experiments show reward quality changes whether RL improves, stalls, or degrades performance [5]. Second, PRMs may provide richer credit assignment but are costly to label and can over-penalize useful steps [2][12]. Third, evaluation must better isolate generalization from memorization, and accuracy from test-time compute; VerifyBench’s challenging subset shows that verifier accuracy on ordinary cases does not remove the need to test harder cases [6]. Finally, capability evaluations do not replace deployment safeguards: the o1 system card reports a chain-of-thought deception monitor validated at 92% accuracy on a small internal on-policy dataset, but cautions that the 100,000 prompts it monitored were synthetic and that the monitor was model-based [8]. Robust progress therefore requires reporting model and sampling budgets, reward/verifier accuracy and cost, clean out-of-distribution results, trace quality, and safety limitations alongside headline benchmark scores [5][6][8].
