# Video and Multimodal Generation: Methods, Evaluation, and Open Challenges

## TL;DR
- Video generators have converged on compressed spatiotemporal representations, but their sequence models differ: VideoGPT predicts discrete video tokens autoregressively, while diffusion systems iteratively denoise video latents or patches.[1][2][3]
- Extending image-generation systems with video data and temporal layers proved a practical path to text-to-video; the newer frontier emphasizes longer clips, motion control, and joint audio-video output.[4][5][6]
- Evaluation is shifting from broad similarity scores toward fine-grained, human-aligned checks: VBench uses 16 dimensions, while T2V-CompBench tests seven compositional capabilities over 1,400 prompts.[7][8]
- Strong visual plausibility is not evidence of physical or semantic reliability: the VIDEOPHY study reports the best tested model satisfied both caption and physical-law criteria in only 39.6% of cases.[9]

## Background
Video and multimodal generation covers systems that synthesize temporally ordered visual content from text, images, video, audio, or structured controls, and increasingly systems that generate more than one modality together. Unlike image synthesis, a video model must preserve appearance over time while producing plausible motion and respecting prompt constraints; multimodal generation adds cross-modal consistency, such as correspondence between a visible event and its sound. Video diffusion research explicitly frames temporal coherence and fidelity as core goals, and surveys conditioning regimes spanning text, images, videos, and audio.[2][10]

Two representation choices established much of the field. VideoGPT compresses clips into discrete codes with a VQ-VAE built from 3D convolutions and axial self-attention, then uses a GPT-like transformer to autoregressively predict the codes.[1] Diffusion models instead learn to reverse a noise process; early video diffusion work adapted image-generation architectures and reported that joint image-video training reduced minibatch-gradient variance and sped optimization.[2] These approaches trade off a token-by-token sequence factorization against iterative denoising, with compression used to make the spatiotemporal computation tractable.[1][2]

The data strategy also shaped text-to-video. Make-A-Video combined paired text-image data for semantic correspondence with unpaired video for motion learning, sidestepping the need for paired text-video examples; its reported interpolation pipeline expands a 16-frame sample to 76 frames.[4] The scale and limits of datasets matter: WebVid-10M contains 10.7 million clips (about 52,000 hours), while HD-VILA-100M is described as over 100 million clips; UCF101, with over 13,000 clips and 101 action labels, remains a benchmark dataset.[10] Sora's report illustrates a later latent-patch approach—compressing visual data into spacetime tokens for a diffusion transformer—and describes up to one-minute high-fidelity generation, while noting that implementation details are not disclosed.[3]

## Representations and generative backbones
Autoregressive and diffusion methods both operate on compressed video, but their generation dynamics differ. VideoGPT makes video a discrete sequence modeling problem: the VQ-VAE supplies a compact codebook, and the transformer predicts codes in spatiotemporal order.[1] VideoPoet generalizes this pattern to a decoder-only transformer trained on multimodal generative objectives, handling images, video, text, and audio inputs and describing zero-shot video generation, including video with matching audio.[11] Such sequence formulations make multimodal token integration natural, but require modeling long sequences of tokens.[1][11]

Latent diffusion substitutes iterative denoising for next-token prediction. Video Diffusion Models extends standard image diffusion, and Latte makes the latent representation explicit by extracting spatiotemporal tokens and using transformer blocks; its four variants decompose spatial and temporal dimensions to manage token volume.[2][12] Sora likewise describes a diffusion transformer that predicts clean spacetime patches from noisy patches and conditioning such as text.[3] Compared with the discrete autoregressive framing, these diffusion-transformer designs center denoising over continuous compressed patches; both approaches still depend on representation compression and temporal modeling.[1][12][3]

A related practical divide concerns duration. Hierarchical latent video diffusion targets longer clips through low-dimensional 3D latents and hierarchical diffusion, while FreeLong and LongDiff extend short-video diffusion systems without retraining.[13][5][14] FreeLong reports taking a model trained on 16 frames to 128 frames with SpectralBlend Temporal Attention; LongDiff instead introduces Position Mapping and Informative Frame Selection to address position ambiguity and information constraints.[5][14] These approaches reduce the need to train a long-video model from scratch, but their distinct mechanisms underscore that maintaining useful temporal structure—not simply increasing frame count—is the central extension problem.[5][14]

## Control, duration, and multimodal output
Control systems differ in what they expose to users. Control-A-Video conditions text-to-video diffusion on control signals using spatiotemporal attention and residual noise initialization.[15] MotionCtrl separates camera-motion and object-motion modules, allowing camera poses and object trajectories to be controlled independently or together; its authors also note the lack of a dataset jointly annotating captions, camera poses, and object trajectories.[16] The contrast is between conditioning a generation process on signals and explicitly disentangling motion factors, with annotation scarcity limiting richer supervised control.[15][16]

Efficiency and duration have likewise become architectural targets. LTX-Video combines a Video-VAE with a denoising transformer and reports a 1:192 compression ratio, with spatiotemporal downscaling of 32 × 32 × 8 pixels per token.[17] StreamDiT focuses on online generation: its 4B-parameter model reports 16 FPS at 512p on one GPU, illustrating an inference-speed target for video generation.[18] Separately, GigaVideo-1 reports approximately 4% average improvement over Wan2.1 across 17 VBench-2.0 dimensions after four GPU-hours of fine-tuning, using automatic feedback rather than manual annotations.[19]

Multimodal generation is moving from video-only output toward synchronization as a first-class objective. MMAudio generates audio conditioned on video and optional text, using larger text-audio data alongside video data and a frame-level synchronization module for audio latents.[6] SyncFlow instead generates audio and video jointly from text, motivated by information loss in cascaded generation and in approaches using contrastive encoders for conditioning.[20] VideoPoet offers a broader autoregressive multimodal interface, while MMAudio is a video-to-audio approach and SyncFlow targets joint text-to-audiovisual generation.[11][6][20] This is a shift from borrowing alignment across separate models to modeling alignment inside the generation process; evaluation results still indicate that synchronization and audio realism remain open weaknesses.[21]

## Evaluation, trustworthiness, and physical consistency
Evaluation has progressed from global scores toward diagnostic dimensions. FETV uses 619 prompts, including 78 manually written unusual prompts, and collects 28,116 human ratings across static quality, temporal quality, text-video alignment, and fine-grained attribute alignment; its tested systems struggled with action quality and controlling quantity, motion direction, and event order.[22] The study reports weak human correlation for FID, FVD, and CLIPScore, while UMTScore and FVD-UMT correlate better in its testbed.[22] VBench complements this prompt-based protocol with 16 dimensions, around 100 prompts per dimension, and paired human preference judgments targeted at a particular dimension.[7] These benchmarks give more actionable diagnoses than a single score, but the results remain tied to the prompts, models, and dimensions actually tested.[22][7]

Compositional and multimodal evaluation sharpen this trend. T2V-CompBench uses 1,400 prompts across seven categories—including attribute binding, spatial relationships, motion binding, object interactions, and numeracy—and combines MLLM-, detection-, and tracking-based metrics.[8] VBench++ extends the fine-grained approach to image-to-video and adds trustworthiness dimensions, including an adaptive-aspect-ratio image suite.[23] For text-to-audio-video, T2AV-Compass evaluates 11 representative systems on 500 complex prompts using signal-level video/audio/alignment measures plus judge-based instruction following and realism; it reports persistent weaknesses in audio realism, fine-grained synchronization, and instruction following.[21] The progression is from visual quality and prompt alignment to specific compositional and cross-modal correctness, which generic embedding similarity does not necessarily capture.[7][8][21]

Safety and physics are not reducible to visual quality. T2VSafetyBench tests nine text-to-video systems across four primary categories and 14 critical aspects, with a 689-prompt subset; it identifies temporal risk that may only emerge across frames and finds no model excels across every safety dimension.[24] For physical reasoning, VIDEOPHY tests caption following and physical-law consistency in videos involving material interactions; CogVideoX-5B, the best tested model, achieved both in just 39.6% of instances.[9] PhyGenBench covers 160 prompts spanning 27 physical laws and four domains, and reports that scaling model size or prompt engineering alone is insufficient in dynamic scenarios.[25] Together these tests challenge the inference that a convincing frame sequence is a reliable simulator of actions or a safe response to a prompt.[24][9][25]

## Trends and open problems
From 2024 through 2025, the visible shift is from making plausible short clips toward longer, more controllable and multimodal sequences. Latte and Sora represent transformer-based latent or patch diffusion; FreeLong and LongDiff test ways to extend short-video models; MMAudio and SyncFlow directly address audiovisual synchronization.[12][3][5][14][6][20] Benchmarks have also expanded from broad quality scores to temporal, compositional, trustworthiness, physics, and cross-modal evaluation.[22][23][8][21][9][25] Real-time generation is an additional objective, with StreamDiT reporting 16 FPS at 512p, yet its reported 64-GPU distillation underscores the difference between fast inference and inexpensive development.[18]

Several problems remain unresolved. Fine-grained control is constrained by the absence of comprehensive training annotations, as MotionCtrl's camera/object-control setup makes explicit.[16] Long-video systems must preserve identity, event order, and useful motion as duration grows; benchmark weaknesses in event order and motion direction show that these are not solved by perceptual quality alone.[5][14][22] For audio-video, synchronized generation is a modeling goal, but benchmarked systems still show synchronization and realism gaps.[20][21] Finally, physical commonsense, semantic reliability, and temporal safety call for evaluations beyond aesthetic ratings and for generation systems whose controls make their failure modes inspectable.[24][9][25] Current evidence does not establish that larger models or more persuasive appearance will resolve those failures; a key research need is to improve measurable temporal, physical, and cross-modal reliability alongside fidelity.[22][21][25]
